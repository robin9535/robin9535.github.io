River oxygen sag

Watch what a waste discharge does to the oxygen in a river. Change the flow, the pollution load and the temperature, then follow a parcel of water downstream to see where dissolved oxygen drops lowest. This is the classic Streeter–Phelps model from environmental engineering.

How it works

  • Mixing. Just below the outfall the river and waste mix completely. Flow-weighted averages give the starting BOD L₀, oxygen DO₀ and temperature.
  • Oxygen demand. Bacteria use oxygen to break down the waste: L(t) = L₀·e−kdt.
  • Oxygen deficit. D = DOsat − DO. It grows from deoxygenation and shrinks from reaeration through the water surface: D(t) = [kdL₀ / (kr − kd)]·(e−kdt − e−krt) + D₀·e−krt.
  • Critical point. The lowest oxygen occurs at tc = [1 / (kr − kd)] · ln{ (kr/kd) · [1 − D₀(kr − kd) / (kdL₀)] }. Distance = velocity × time.
  • Temperature. Rates are corrected as kT = k20·θT−20 with θ = 1.047 for deoxygenation and 1.024 for reaeration. Saturation DO comes from a standard freshwater table (0–35 °C, sea level) by interpolation.

Rates used here are typical textbook values and defaults are for learning. Real rivers need measured kd and kr. The model assumes one steady discharge, a single straight reach, no algae, sediment demand or tributaries, so use it to understand cause and effect, not to permit a real discharge. If DO would fall to zero the model's equations stop being valid and the page says so.
